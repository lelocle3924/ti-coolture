Your browser doesn't support animation-timeline — the demos below will render statically. Chrome, Edge or Safari 26+ will show them moving.
The finding that changes the cost calculation Most awwwards-style scroll work used to mean GSAP ScrollTrigger, Lenis and a lot of JavaScript running on the main thread. That's no longer true. Scroll-driven animations are now native CSS — animation-timeline: scroll() and view() — supported in Chrome/Edge 115+ and Safari 26, with Firefox still behind a flag as of mid-2026. Around 84–85% globally, and higher than that in Vietnam where mobile Chrome and Safari dominate.

Two consequences that matter for you specifically. First, these run on the compositor thread, so they stay smooth even on a budget Android where main-thread JavaScript would stutter — and 87% of your traffic is mobile. Second, unsupported browsers simply ignore animation-timeline and show the end state, so with animation-fill-mode: both the fallback is "no animation", not "broken layout". That makes almost everything in Tier 1 a genuinely safe bet on your timeline.

↔ swipe or drag horizontally — native momentum, zero JavaScript

This is what should power both the district carousel (MAP1) and the What's In Store row (HP-03). No Swiper, no Embla, no touch handlers.

.snap{
  display:flex; overflow-x:auto;
  scroll-snap-type:x mandatory;
  scroll-padding:12px;
}
.snap>div{
  flex:0 0 84%;
  scroll-snap-align:start;
  scroll-snap-stop:always;
}

scroll-snap-stop:always is the detail people miss: without it a fast flick skips three districts at once. Note the 84% width — the peek of the next card is the swipe affordance, which is why the wireframe shows a partial third card.

One conflict to handle: a horizontal snap area sitting inside a vertically-scrolling page will occasionally swallow a diagonal thumb swipe. Your arrow buttons aren't decoration — they're the reliable path. HP-03 HP-04

This is the effect that makes an MPA feel like an app, and it costs almost nothing. Cross-document view transitions ship in Chrome/Edge 126+ and Safari 18.2+; Firefox is still in development. Unsupported browsers just navigate normally.

Your SRS bans a SPA (D3, correctly — SEO is the growth channel). Historically that meant accepting hard cuts between pages as the price of server rendering. It no longer does.

/* both pages, in the global stylesheet */
@view-transition{ navigation:auto; }

/* the shared element, on both pages */
.product-image{
  view-transition-name:product-hero;
}

The name must be unique among visible elements — two cards sharing product-hero throws and skips the transition. Generate it per product: view-transition-name:product-{slug}, set on the grid card and on the detail page's main image.

Where I'd use it: product card → product page (the morph above); shop card → shop page; and a directional slide on blog topic navigation. That's three transitions covering most of the site's movement.

↕ scroll vertically — the track moves sideways, section pinned

The signature awwwards move, and achievable with position:sticky plus a scroll-linked translate — still no JavaScript.

Why Tier 2 rather than Tier 1. It hijacks the meaning of a scroll gesture, which some people find disorienting, and it needs a tall wrapper (about 3 screens here) that inflates page height and confuses scroll-progress indicators. It also makes the pinned content harder to reach by keyboard.

If you use it, use it once — as a category showcase on the homepage, not on a browse page where people are trying to get somewhere. And give it a visible progress indicator so the pin doesn't feel like a freeze.

Tier 3 · the awwwards look you should decline

Smooth-scroll hijacking (Lenis, Locomotive) This is the single most common ingredient in awwwards sites and the one I'd most strongly avoid here. It replaces the browser's native scroll with a JS-interpolated one, and that breaks four things you've already committed to: scroll-snap stops working reliably, your header hide/show fights the interpolation, the reveal footer's sticky positioning gets unpredictable, and native scroll performance on budget Android drops noticeably. It also degrades find-in-page and screen-reader navigation. The irony is that CSS scroll-driven animations already give you the smoothness people are chasing with these libraries, without taking the scroll away from the user.
Per-character text animation on Vietnamese Splitting headlines into per-glyph spans with overflow:hidden masks is standard practice in these showcases — and it's a trap in Vietnamese. Stacked diacritics (ế, ộ, ữ) sit outside the nominal em box and get clipped by exactly the masks that make the effect work. You've already hit this once with line-height 0.88. If you want animated headlines, mask at word or line level with generous vertical padding, and test on Tuyển chọn có "gu" and Nhà cửa before committing.
WebGL image distortion, cursor-followers, custom cursors Three.js hover-distortion and cursor trails are a large part of what makes those sites look like those sites. They're also desktop-only effects, and 87% of your traffic is mobile — so you'd be spending your scarcest resource on 13% of your visitors. Add the bundle size against your LCP target and it's a clear no for V1.
Scroll-triggered autoplay video and full-bleed video heroes Heavy on roaming data, which is exactly Daniel's situation, and hostile to your LCP budget. If you want motion in the hero, the image loop you've already specified does the job at a fraction of the weight.
A page-load intro animation The "TÍ" wordmark drawing itself before the site appears is tempting given your art direction. Don't: it delays LCP by design, it's infuriating on the second visit, and for a site whose growth channel is people arriving from Google on a specific product, an intro is a toll booth in front of the thing they came for.

If you only do four things

Recommended V1 scope, roughly a day and a half of work
  1. Cross-document view transitions (#07) — one at-rule plus per-product names. Biggest perceived-quality jump available to you, and it directly answers "SSR sites feel dated".
  2. Scroll-snap carousels (#06) — replaces a carousel library you'd otherwise ship, on two features you're already committed to.
  3. Staggered reveal below the fold (#02) — highest polish per line of CSS.
  4. Reveal footer with a scroll-linked wordmark (#05) — you're building the footer anyway; the wordmark is about ten extra lines and it's where your art direction and your scroll behaviour meet.
Everything above degrades to a static page on unsupported browsers and to no motion under reduced-motion. None of it blocks server rendering, none of it touches your LCP path, and none of it requires a JavaScript animation library — which matters because a beginner developer debugging GSAP timelines nine days before launch is a schedule risk you don't need.
One thing to test before committing Scroll-snap, a hide-on-scroll header, a sticky ORDER NOW bar and a reveal footer all manipulate the same scroll surface. Individually each is fine. Together, on a real mid-range Android in Chrome, they can interact in ways that don't show up in a desktop browser — the classic symptom is the header flickering at a snap boundary. Build the product page first with all four active, and check it on a physical device before you extend the pattern to the rest of the site.
